01 · About me

I got interested in startups through ambition. I stayed because of bottlenecks.

The most interesting problems were never motivational. They were structural. Why does one person compound and another stay busy forever?

01

I'm building Empy because founders usually don't lose to lack of ideas. They lose to dropped context.

02

I care about product, AI, and execution because that's where good intentions either turn into work or evaporate.

03

I'm from Armenia. That means I never assumed the center of the world would invite me in.

02 · How I'm wired

I was never interested in technology as fashion.

I like hidden structure. Why one person gets a lot more from the same day. Why one company gets calmer as it grows and another gets noisier. Why some work compounds and some work leaks away.

I like finding the real constraint.
I trust patterns more than hype.
People call some of this product taste. A lot of it is just seeing structure.
03 · What shaped me

Games taught me more than school did.

What I got from games

  • Most people play the visible game. The real game is one layer deeper.
  • Tradeoffs matter more than looking talented.
  • Small advantages get big if you keep using them.

Why it mattered later

Startups are like that too. You win by seeing something earlier than other people, then staying with it long enough for it to matter.

04 · My first founder mode

My first founder operating system was simple: push harder.

Fast

I could move fast, improvise, and outwork people for a while.

Messy

I kept too much in my head and called that control.

Fragile

It looked strong from the outside, but it did not compound. If I got overloaded, everything slowed down.

Intensity helps. It is not a system.

05 · Turning point

I realized I was not underpowered. I was badly organized.

What started breaking

  • Too many open loops in my head
  • Context spread across chats, docs, tools, and memory
  • Execution quality changed with my energy

The actual lesson

A lot of founders say they want leverage, then build a company that depends on them remembering everything.

06 · What changed

I stopped treating the problem as motivation. It was architecture.

MemoryGood systems remember, so the founder does not have to.
WorkflowsWork should move through a path, not bounce around your head.
OrchestrationWrite it down. Execute. Review. Repeat.
Feedback loopsReality teaches faster than demos do.

I did not need more tools. I needed a system that made good decisions easier to repeat.

07 · How I build now

Now I try to build in a way that survives bad days.

01

Use structure where structure helps. Use models where flexibility helps.

02

Test on real inputs. Fake demos teach fake lessons.

03

Ship in small steps. Big plans are often fear wearing a suit.

04

Cheap speed is expensive later.

I am not trying to look impressive for two days. I am trying to become useful for a long time.

08 · Where I’m going

AI gets interesting when it stops acting like a vending machine.

Less dropped context
More follow through
Memory that persists
Systems that improve with use

Most AI products still work like this: you type something in, something comes out, and next time you start over. That is useful, but shallow. I care about systems that remember what matters and get better as they work with you.

09 · What is a startup?

A startup is not a status symbol. It is a search.

01

A normal business runs a known playbook. A startup is looking for one.

02

If you find one, the upside can be huge. That is why ambitious people do it.

03

It also means long stretches where nobody can tell whether you are early or just wrong.

10 · Two ways to build

There are two honest ways to play.

Normal business

  • You use a model that already works
  • You get to revenue faster
  • You keep more control
  • Usually less upside and less chaos

Venture startup

  • You chase something unproven but scalable
  • You tolerate more uncertainty for more upside
  • You often take investor money and lose some freedom
  • If it works, it can work much bigger

Neither is morally superior. Just do not lie to yourself about which game you are playing.

11 · Over to you

Three questions I would ask if we were being honest.

  1. What problem do you care about enough to stay with after the novelty dies?
  2. Do you actually want the startup path, or just the image of it?
  3. Are you waiting to feel ready, or are you willing to start before that happens?

You probably do not need more advice. You need a more honest answer.
