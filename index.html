I used to think raw intensity was enough. It wasn't.

This is the story of what broke, what changed, and why I'm building systems instead of relying on adrenaline.

Building Empy
01 · About me

I'm a founder who got obsessed with the bottleneck.

The bottleneck turned out to be me: my memory, my context switching, my tendency to run the company out of my own nervous system.

01

I'm building Empy because founders drown in fragmentation long before they run out of ideas.

02

I care about product, AI, and execution systems because that's where work either compounds or disappears.

03

I'm from Armenia. The internet lets you build from the edge — if you're good enough.

02 · How I'm wired

I was never interested in technology as fashion.

What interests me is hidden structure: why one person gets 10x output from the same day, why one company compounds and another stays frantic, why some work gets easier with time and some work gets harder.

I like finding the real constraint.
I trust patterns more than hype.
People call it product taste. A lot of it is systems thinking.
03 · What shaped me

Games taught me more about startups than school did.

What I got from games

  • Most people play the visible game. The real game is the meta.
  • Tradeoffs matter more than talent theatre.
  • A small edge, used repeatedly, becomes unfair.

Why it mattered later

Startups are like that too. The advantage usually goes to the person who sees the structure early and is stubborn enough to keep playing when it still looks small.

04 · My first founder mode

My first founder operating system was: don't stop.

Fast

I could move fast, improvise, and outwork people for short stretches.

Messy

I kept too much in my head and mistook that for control.

Fragile

It looked strong from the outside, but it didn't compound. If I got overloaded, everything slowed down.

Intensity is real. It's just a bad long-term architecture.

05 · Turning point

I realized I wasn't underpowered. I was badly organized.

What started breaking

  • Too many half-open loops in my head
  • Context scattered across chats, docs, tools, and memory
  • Execution quality rose and fell with my energy

The actual lesson

A lot of founders say they want leverage. Then they build a company that depends on them remembering everything.

06 · What changed

I stopped treating the problem like motivation. It was architecture.

MemoryGood systems remember, so the founder doesn't have to.
WorkflowsWork should move through a path, not bounce around your brain.
OrchestrationSpec → execution → review beats heroic improvisation.
Feedback loopsReality is better than demos. Real use teaches you what matters.

I didn't need more tools. I needed a system that made good decisions easier to repeat.

07 · How I build now

Now I try to build in a way that survives my bad days.

01

Use structure where structure helps. Use models where flexibility helps.

02

Test on real inputs. Fake demos teach fake lessons.

03

Ship in increments. Big dramatic plans are usually procrastination with better branding.

04

Care about quality. Cheap speed is expensive later.

I'm not trying to be impressive for two days. I'm trying to become dangerous for ten years.

08 · Where I’m going

I think AI gets interesting when it becomes part of the operating system.

Less dropped context
More follow-through
Memory that persists
Systems that get smarter with use

Most AI products feel like vending machines. You put in a prompt, something comes out. That's useful, but it's not enough. I care about systems that accumulate context and become better collaborators over time.

09 · What is a startup?

A startup is not a vibe. It's a search.

01

A normal business runs a known playbook. A startup is looking for one that works.

02

That search can create huge upside — which is why it attracts ambitious people.

03

It also means confusion, wrong turns, and long periods where the outside world can't tell if you're early or just lost.

10 · Two ways to build

There are two honest games here.

Normal business

  • You use a model that already works
  • You get to revenue faster
  • You keep more control
  • Usually less upside, less chaos

Venture startup

  • You chase something unproven but scalable
  • You tolerate more uncertainty for more upside
  • You often take investor money and lose some freedom
  • If it works, it works much bigger

Neither is morally superior. Just don't lie to yourself about which game you're in.

11 · Over to you

Three questions I'd ask if we were talking honestly.

  1. What problem do you care about enough to stay with after the novelty dies?
  2. Do you actually want the startup path — or just the image of it?
  3. Are you waiting for confidence first, or are you willing to start before you have it?

You probably don't need more advice. You need a more honest answer.
